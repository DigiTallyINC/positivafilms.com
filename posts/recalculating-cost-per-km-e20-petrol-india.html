Recalculating Your Cost Per Kilometre on E20: A 10-Minute Refresh Every Driver Should Do

Jul 29, 2026 7 Min Read

E20 became the only petrol at Indian pumps on 1 April 2026, and if you haven't refreshed your running-cost number since then, you're budgeting from a figure that belongs to a fuel that no longer exists.

Most drivers have a rough sense of their car's mileage. Fewer have a precise sense of what each kilometre actually costs them. And almost nobody has updated that figure since the fuel changed.

That's the gap this covers. Not theory, not averages from a test agency: your number, for your vehicle, on the fuel that's currently in your tank.

Why Your Old ₹/km Number Is Now Stale

Before April 2026, petrol at Indian pumps was a mix of blends, with E10 being the predominant standard in most cities. Since 1 April 2026, every pump in India dispenses E20: 20% ethanol, minimum RON 95. There is no lower blend available at retail. You cannot opt out of it.

Two things changed simultaneously. First, the fuel's energy density dropped slightly compared to E10, which means your engine extracts marginally less energy from each litre burned. Government estimates put the mileage effect at roughly 1–2% for vehicles calibrated for E20, and up to 6–7% for older vehicles calibrated for earlier blends. Controlled test-agency results published in July 2026 measured 2–6% higher fuel consumption versus E10 across vehicles in the 3–10 year age bracket.

Second, fuel prices have continued to move. The retail price per litre you used when you last calculated your ₹/km may be anywhere from a few paise to a couple of rupees away from today's price, depending on your city and how long ago you did the math.

Put those two together: a changed fuel and a changed price, and your old cost-per-km is built on two assumptions that are both out of date. The fix takes ten minutes if you have your fill-up records. If you don't have records, this is the moment to start keeping them.

The Calculation, Laid Out Simply

Cost per kilometre has exactly one correct formula:

₹/km = Total ₹ spent on fuel ÷ Total km driven

That's it. No engine displacement correction. No "claimed mileage" adjustment. No manufacturer figure involved anywhere. The only inputs are money out of your wallet and kilometres on your odometer.

The trap most people fall into is using a single fill-up as the basis. One fill-up gives you mileage, not cost per kilometre, and even that mileage number is unreliable unless you're doing it tank-to-tank correctly (more on the method in the tank-to-tank guide). For cost per kilometre, you need at least three to five fill-ups, preferably more, to smooth out the variation that comes from traffic, load, AC usage, and the fact that no two fill-ups land at exactly the same fuel level.

A Worked Example With Real Numbers

Say you drive a mass-market hatchback in Pune. Over five fill-ups spanning 2,200 km since April 2026, you spent ₹8,470 on petrol. Your ₹/km is:

₹8,470 ÷ 2,200 km = ₹3.85 per km

Now compare that to what you calculated last year on E10 at a different price per litre. If your old figure was ₹3.50/km, the difference is ₹0.35 per kilometre. On a 1,500 km monthly driving pattern, that's ₹525 per month more than you budgeted: ₹6,300 per year, sitting invisibly inside a number you thought you knew.

That's not a dramatic amount, but it's not nothing either. More importantly, it's a real number from your actual driving, not an estimate from a forum post about E20's "theoretical" impact.

The City Factor

If you drive in Bengaluru or Delhi or Mumbai's peak hours, your E20 ₹/km is going to differ meaningfully from the same vehicle doing highway runs between Ahmedabad and Surat. Stop-start traffic punishes fuel economy harder now than it did on E10, because ethanol blends lose more efficiency at partial throttle than at steady cruise. This is not a reason to panic; it's a reason to track your city-driving fill-ups separately from your highway fill-ups if you do both regularly, so your budget figure actually reflects how you drive.

How to Run the Refresh

Step one: gather your last five to eight fill-up receipts or memory-bank entries. If you've been keeping digital records, pull the data. If you haven't, start now and come back to this calculation after four fill-ups.

Step two: add up the total rupees spent across those fill-ups. Don't include anything except fuel: no servicing, no oil, no accessories. Pure fuel spend only.

Step three: subtract the odometer reading at the first fill-up from the odometer reading at the last fill-up. That's your kilometre base.

Step four: divide. ₹ total divided by km total. Write that number down somewhere you'll see it again in three months.

Step five: cross-check it against your monthly driving. If you drive roughly 1,200 km a month, multiply your ₹/km by 1,200. Is that monthly fuel budget number higher than what you've been assuming? By how much? That gap is the number that matters for your actual household or vehicle budget.

How Often Should You Recalculate

Fuel prices at Indian pumps are not fixed. They shift with global crude, with state taxes, and occasionally with central policy. A ₹/km figure calculated in April 2026 will be slightly different from one calculated in October 2026 even if your mileage stays perfectly constant, purely because the price per litre has moved.

A reasonable rhythm: recalculate your cost per kilometre every three months. If petrol prices change by more than ₹2 per litre between recalculations, do it sooner. If you've recently had your vehicle serviced (new air filter, tyre pressure corrected, injector cleaning), recalculate after four fill-ups to see whether the service actually moved your mileage number. Your ₹/km is a living figure, not a one-time answer.

The fuel ₹/km calculated here is the piece that changes most frequently and is therefore the one worth refreshing regularly.

What to Do If Your Number Has Gone Up

If your recalculated E20 ₹/km is materially higher than your old figure, there are a few things worth checking before assuming it's all the fuel:

Tyre pressure first. Under-inflated tyres add rolling resistance and lift fuel consumption noticeably. Check cold pressure against the sticker on your door jamb or fuel flap, not the tyre sidewall maximum. This is free to fix and surprisingly often overlooked after monsoon temperature swings change tyre pressure without any puncture.

Air filter second. A clogged filter restricts airflow, richens the mixture, and drops mileage. If you haven't had it checked in two service cycles, have it inspected.

Driving pattern third. If you've started a new commute, changed your regular route, or the city's traffic has genuinely worsened (Bengaluru's outer ring road at 6 PM is a different fuel burn than it was two years ago), your pattern has changed and the higher number may simply be accurate.

Fuel quality is occasionally raised as a concern on forums, but it's not a variable you can measure at home. What you can measure is your own fill-up data: if your mileage drops suddenly at one specific pump and recovers at others, that's worth noting. Otherwise, focus on the variables you can actually control.

Once you've checked those items and have a clean four-fill-up baseline post-E20, that number is your new ₹/km. Budget from it. Revisit it in three months. The fuel has changed permanently; the habit of measuring it should change to match.
